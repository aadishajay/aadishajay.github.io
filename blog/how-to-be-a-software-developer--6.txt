1d:T1f63,<p>Ten years in software industry is nothing, but it has given me quite an insight on what the industry looks for in a developer. I would try to share these things out of my experience, and as a word of caution, the opinions are highly personal.</p>
<h2>How I started?</h2>
<p><img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/163444/sport-treadmill-tor-route-163444.jpeg?auto=compress&#x26;cs=tinysrgb&#x26;w=1260&#x26;h=750&#x26;dpr=1" alt=""></p>
<blockquote>
<p>Photo by <a href="https://unsplash.com/@bradencollum">Braden Collum</a></p>
</blockquote>
<p>I was a campus hire, which I consider myself to be too lucky. I had studied photonics, a branch of physics at the Cochin University of Science and Technology. I got hired to Tata Consultancy Services in my penultimate semester. Since, I was from a non-technical background (that’s what they called the non-CS people) , the interview questions were more or less based on the minimum expectation level. Somehow it went well, and I crash-landed to the industry. I had 3 papers of Computer science in my syllabus, two of which basic Computer science more or less of C Programming and then one of Matlab. I had a small background of C programming during my high school days and, LOGOS and BASIC programming in my primary school. These had given me a minimal understanding of programming.</p>
<p>Later in the start of my professional journey, I had a 3 month of training at TCS. I was taught HTML/CSS/JavaScript, then Java , SQL , Informatica and BI Tools. After training, in my first project I was hired for a team that was supporting a client who was using Oracle Retail. Oracle Retail used SQL, PL/SQL , Oracle Forms , Oracle BI. I was chosen as developer who would develop solutions in most of these technologies, but primary ones were SQL and PL/SQL.</p>
<p>I had near zero experience on these, but had invested in learning, putting in extra hours if required. I had the luxury of being surrounded by highly motivated, skilled team members and leads who helped me out every instance whenever I was stuck. Every new company and project I had switched over the years, I had encountered new technologies and I tried learning them without much of hesitation. I had found everyone who mentored me supportive and coping up with the fact that I was new to this and still learning.
 </p>
<h2>What does the industry expects?</h2>
<p><img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1538766017398-415434a31a5b?crop=entropy&#x26;cs=tinysrgb&#x26;fm=jpg&#x26;ixlib=rb-1.2.1&#x26;q=80&#x26;raw_url=true&#x26;ixid=MnwxMjA3fDB8MHxwaG90by1wYWdlfHx8fGVufDB8fHx8&#x26;auto=format&#x26;fit=crop&#x26;w=1740" alt=""></p>
<blockquote>
<p>Photo by <a href="https://unsplash.com/@craft_ear">Jan Tinneberg</a></p>
</blockquote>
<p>Out of my experience these are the traits that the industry looks out for.</p>
<ul>
<li> Being strong in the technology you are working on. The expectation is as per the years of experience you have. This means that you should be constantly upscaling yourselves with newer versions of the tools/languages you work on.</li>
<li>Open to learn technologies that you don’t know. These technologies needn’t be modern and cutting edge, but what the project demands.</li>
<li>Effective in communicating things verbally as well as in writing. This surely will take you a long way. Openly expressing your opinions and communicating the roadblocks would definitely help.</li>
<li>Problem solving skills. Ultimately, every software development task boils down into finding the logic to solve the problem. Major chunk of software development work is just all about finding a preexisting solution and putting it in the right place.</li>
<li>Ability to find problems in existing solutions, finding the solution for those and being able to put forth these to the team or your manager.</li>
<li>Team player. Until and unless you are a freelancer who is directly talking to client, taking down the requirement and single handedly working on those – you will have to work with a team. And when working in a team you will have to display a few qualities, like being able to compromise, listen to others, etc. And believe me, nobody works solo in this industry.
 </li>
</ul>
<h2>Where to start?</h2>
<p><img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1589953605483-a2964c38d415?ixlib=rb-1.2.1&#x26;raw_url=true&#x26;q=80&#x26;fm=jpg&#x26;crop=entropy&#x26;cs=tinysrgb&#x26;ixid=MnwxMjA3fDB8MHxwaG90by1wYWdlfHx8fGVufDB8fHx8&#x26;auto=format&#x26;fit=crop&#x26;w=1742" alt=""></p>
<blockquote>
<p>Photo by <a href="https://unsplash.com/@markusspiske">Markus Spiske</a></p>
</blockquote>
<h3>First small steps</h3>
<p>I would suggest that you should check with yourselves if you really are a developer material. There is only one correct way to do that – try writing code. But that doesn’t end up there. You will have to find some problems around you and try solving using code. You may fail to achieve a perfect solution, but no learning would be wasted.</p>
<p>To start coding, you needn’t worry that you need an expensive computer – try to get an old one, or even there are alternatives such as using mobile phone or cheaper computers like the Raspberry Pi , or at worst writing code on a notepad – but the main thing is writing code! You will find a lot of guidance over the internet.</p>
<h3>Showing it off</h3>
<p>Once you are confident enough, you can start show-casing your talent. You may create a portfolio website, not necessarily hosted on your own domain – you may use free alternatives like GitHub Pages. Let the world see what you have got. This will help your code being scrutinised by others.</p>
<p>You may leverage your existing social media accounts for this, a LinkedIn profile will surely help as it meant solely for professional networking. You might get some disheartening reviews as well, if that catches your nerve, take a small break, and go through those reviews again after the break and most of them might even have suggestions on how to fix it. Also be courageous enough to brush off negative reviews that offer no solutions and equally learn to ignore that review from your friend who would say “Wow, Great” to everything you write unless they mean to.</p>
<h3>Getting help, every single time</h3>
<p>The more code you write, the more issues you will face. And believe me, those hindrances are the steppingstones in your career. You should try solving them by googling them, and in most cases, you will be astonished to find that you are not the first to stumble upon such a error. Sites like Stack Exchange will have the answer.
 </p>
<h3>Be ready to be inspired, shamelessly</h3>
<p>Just realise that you are not Ken Thompson or Dennis Ritchie trying to create Unix or C programming language – you are learning, and you don’t have to have original ideas. Well as a matter-of-fact Ken and Dennis too had taken inspiration from other existing programming languages.
So, your solutions can consist of pieces of code that are readily available online. The important thing is that you understand what the copied piece of code does.
 </p>
<h3>A good, genuine resume</h3>
<p>Now this is an important fact. Your resume is the first thing that every recruiter goes through. The questions in your interview would mostly based on what you claim in your resume. Make sure you put anything fake, as you would be caught red-handed sooner or later. But make sure that you include all important things in it. – especially your portfolio website or LinkedIn profile link which would give more insights on your capability.
The resume should look good and attractive and should not span beyond 2 pages. You will find a lot of good resume formats online, take one and build your own. In case you feel that you can’t do one yourselves, find a resume creation service online.</p>
<p>I have tried to summarise almost everything on how to start a career in the IT industry as a developer. Best wishes to everyone how are starting the career as developer.  </p>
